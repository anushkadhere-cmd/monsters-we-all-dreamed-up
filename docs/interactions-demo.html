India’s monsters mostly keep watch. 11 of its 28 creatures lean protector, and only 5 are true destroyers. Garuda carries Vishnu. Shesha holds up the world on a thousand heads. Nandi waits at Shiva’s door.

The Norse built theirs to end the world. Only 3 of their 16 protect. Jörmungandr, Fenrir, Sköll and Hati aren’t guarding anything. They’re waiting for Ragnarök.

Greece’s monsters are tests. 13 of its 28 sit near the middle: the Minotaur in his maze, Scylla on her rock, the Sirens on theirs. They don’t want to end the world. They want to stop one hero.

Japan’s monsters are the ones you meet on the road. 11 of its 24 are neither friend nor foe: the kappa in the river, the snow woman at the door, the spider woman in the house. Some even help you, like the fox Kitsune and the mountain Tengu.

Step cards hold the script’s own paragraphs, unchanged and in order (the rest of Chart 1’s paragraphs follow the same way). Pins are a transparent layer on top of the chart, with pointer events off, so the chart’s own hover still works. Pins are placed in percent of the chart box. Chart 1’s corners never move, so pins can point; Chart 2’s layout shifts, so its notes stay in the step cards.

The tightest knot in the web is a story told in every one of these cultures: a giant serpent, and the storm god who kills it. Apollo kills Python. Zeus buries Typhon under a volcano. Indra frees the world’s rivers from Vritra. Susanoo gets Orochi drunk. Thor and Jörmungandr will kill each other at the end of the world.

Then comes the surprise. The most connected creatures in the whole bestiary aren’t serpents at all. They’re Medusa, Lamia and the yakshini, each tied to ten others.

Greece and India share the most: 25 parallels between them. Japan and Scandinavia barely speak, with just 9.

The bands that curl back home are the true originals, and here the two biggest bestiaries part ways. 39% of Greek monsters have no double in any other tradition, the most of the four. In the Hindu tradition, only 21% don’t. Greece invented the most monsters of its own; India’s echo furthest.
